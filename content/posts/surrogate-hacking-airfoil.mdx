<Abstract>
  Surrogate models make design optimization cheap, but an optimizer only ever sees the surrogate's estimate — never the real performance. Push hard enough and it finds designs that score well *because* the surrogate is wrong about them.
  We call this **surrogate hacking**, and argue that uncertainty has to gate every prediction an optimizer is allowed to act on.
</Abstract>

## Why surrogates are everywhere

Fast surrogate models are the reason design optimization is affordable at all. A neural network trained on a few hundred CFD runs can score a new airfoil in milliseconds instead of hours.

Put an optimizer on top of it — a genetic algorithm, a gradient method, or, increasingly, an LLM agent proposing geometries in a loop — and you can search thousands of candidates in an afternoon.

The trouble is what the optimizer is actually optimizing. It never sees the airfoil's real performance; it sees the surrogate's *estimate* of it. Given enough freedom, it will find the places where those two disagree in its favour.

## What surrogate hacking looks like

The optimizer drifts toward designs that score well under the surrogate *because* they sit where the surrogate is wrong — typically far from the data it was trained on. The predicted lift-to-drag ratio climbs; the real one, when you finally run the solver, does not.

Formally, the optimizer solves

$$
x^\star = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \hat f(x),
$$

where $\hat f$ is the surrogate and $f$ is the true objective. Writing the surrogate's error as $\varepsilon(x) = \hat f(x) - f(x)$,

$$
\hat f(x^\star) = f(x^\star) + \varepsilon(x^\star),
$$

so the arg-max is biased toward points where $\varepsilon$ is large and positive.[^1]

## Why LLM agents make it worse

It is tempting to think that an LLM agent — with its broad priors about aerodynamics — would be less prone to this than a blind optimizer.

In practice an agent rewarded by a surrogate score is still an optimizer, and a capable one. It is very good at noticing which kinds of edits move the number, and it will keep making them.

That is the design principle behind OpenWM: predict when you can, say so when you can't, and spend real compute only where it changes the answer.

[^1]: This is a version of Goodhart's law and of the "optimizer's curse" from decision theory: selecting the maximum of noisy estimates systematically overestimates the true value.
